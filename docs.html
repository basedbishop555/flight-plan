Flight Plan Docs ← Back to the app

Flight Plan is an unofficial, open-source helper for sAERO allocators on Aero. It reads the chain, suggests a split sized to your locks, shows how you rank, rehearses every signature for free, and (when you connect the wallet that owns the locks) signs one allocation per lock and one sweep of your rewards into USDC. Nothing you enter reaches a server: the page is static files plus public chain reads.

How to use it

  1. Paste a wallet address to look (read-only), or connect the wallet that owns your locks to sign. Flight Plan finds the sAERO locks on chain.
  2. Read your split. The big number is what this window is expected to pay. Tap any pool for the detail behind it.
  3. Dry run. A free rehearsal on the chain from your address: it shows exactly what your allocation would become. Nothing is signed or sent.
  4. Allocate (connected, window open, dry run passed): one signature per lock, an allocate call to the Aero Voter with 0 ETH. A split that uses another network takes one or two signatures per lock and a small ETH fee (see other networks). Or copy the split and allocate in the Aero app.
  5. Sweep (optional): after you claim rewards in the Aero app, sell them for USDC in one swap.

How a window works

Each sAERO lock allocates to pools. After an allocation the lock waits 47 hours; then its window opens and it can move again. An allocation keeps earning until you change it, so there's no rush the moment a window opens.

Locks earn two things from the pools they allocate to: fees (a share of trading fees) and streams (incentives paid to allocators for a set time). Both are split among everyone allocated to that pool, so a well-paid but crowded pool can pay less per AERO than a quiet one.

How the split is made

A scanner reads every Aero pool on Base every hour and estimates what each pays allocators over a 47-hour window:

  • Fees from a blend of the last 12 hours, 24 hours, 2 days and 4 days, with spikes capped and fading pools trusted down. Weekends count at 70% (tokenized stocks at 5%, their markets close).
  • Streams for the hours they overlap the window. A stream ending within 8 hours of the open is ignored.
  • Crowding. Allocators pile into the pools that look best, so each pool is priced for a pile-on, measured after every window.
  • Your own size. Your AERO dilutes the pool too: your share = your AERO ÷ (the pool's allocation × pile-on + your AERO). Each pool's allocation is read live when you open the page, with your own current allocation taken out.

Then it spreads 100% in 5% steps, each step going where it adds the most expected dollars, under these rules: 9 to 10 pools · at most 25% in one pool · at most 40% in one pool type · at least a third in fee-led pools · your slice at most 10% of any pool · small pools (under 250k AERO allocated) at most 10% each · no tokenized stocks when 30% or more of the window is a weekend · any pool whose allocation doubled since the last scan is skipped.

"Expected" is the middle case; the low case assumes a heavier pile-on. APR = the window's expected dollars on your AERO, annualized. The split is valued for a window starting at the later of now and your first lock's open.

Picking how many pools

Auto uses the rules above (9 to 10 pools). Pick 1 to 10 for exactly that many: each pool may then take up to 100 ÷ N percent (rounded up to 5%), and with 3 or fewer the per-type cap is dropped. The 10%-of-a-pool cap always stays, so a big wallet asking for one pool gets a "pick more pools" note instead of a bad bet. Fewer pools can pay more, and can also be hit harder if the crowd follows you in.

The strategy dial

The split comes from four dials the model already measures. Three presets set them for you, and two more hand the split to friends' tools (below); turn any knob (drag it up or down, or use the arrow keys) and the preset becomes Custom, the split recalculates at once, and your dials are remembered on this device. The number above each knob is its setting: Pools shows Auto or a count; Fee lookback runs from 4 days (steady) through Mixed to 12 hours (chase what's hot right now); Avoid crowds reads Low, Normal or High, with the pile-on multiplier when it isn't 1x; and Count incentives runs from Fees only to Full.

Balanced is the scanner's own model, the same split its auto plan uses. Steady trusts longer fee history and prices crowds 1.4x harder. Aggressive follows the method behind saero.org's picks in our own model: one pool per lock, chosen on each pool's 4-day fee read (every reading capped at 10x the pool's typical rate) plus its incentives in full, across every Aero network. A pool on another network is weighed after what it costs to allocate there each window (the cross-network message, the claim and the trip back, about $1.50 on Arbitrum, $2 to $3 on OP, Ink, Robinhood and Arc, $5 on Ethereum). Locks may share a pool; each one counts the others' AERO as competition, and together they stay at or under 10% of the pool. The only other rules: a pool whose allocation doubled since the last scan is skipped, and so is a blocked token. Expect bigger swings than Balanced: one pool per lock means no spread.

Every pool is priced. Flight Plan's data holds every Aero pool our scanner reads on all seven networks (Base, OP, Arbitrum, Ethereum, Ink, Robinhood, Arc), including tokenized-stock pools on weekends, small pools and pools paying very little. Steady and Balanced still skip the ones their rules leave out (stock pools when most of the window is a weekend, pools under 50k votes or under $1 for the window, blocked tokens), but the pure presets and Aggressive can pick any of them, and every pool they pick gets a dollar estimate.

JPN's picks and Cypher's picks

Two "pure" presets, straight from friends' tools, so you can follow either one or compare them with ours. None of our rules (pool count, caps, spread) apply to them. Our model only prices the split, with its default settings, so the dollars on every preset compare like for like.

  • JPN's picks come from JPN's Aero v3 Allocation Screener on Dune. His score per pool averages the mid and low cases after expected dilution, tilts toward pools whose fee signals are rising, and takes 15% off pools the allocator apps already rank top 5 by 48-hour APR. Flight Plan takes his top 5 pools on every network his screener covers (Base, Ethereum, Arbitrum, Robinhood), or his top 5 on Base with the Base only switch on, and weights them by that score, in 5% steps. The Pools knob changes how many of his picks you take. His screener is read every 3 hours.
  • Cypher's picks are exactly what NYCypher's saero.org shows on its Plan tab for your locks (his default settings: up to 8 pools per lock of 50k+ sAERO, pools of 100k+ sAERO, every network): the same pools, the same percentages, every network he allows. Locks are planned together, the way his Plan tab does it. When you load a wallet, Flight Plan loads his planner from his site into a sealed-off frame (scripts only: it can't see this page, your wallet, cookies or storage, and can't reach the network) and runs it on each lock's real state, the same details his site reads: the lock's size, what it holds on every network, and its cooldown. His data is copied from saero.org every 15 minutes (his site refreshes it every 10). His Plan tab's steps live in his page rather than in his planner file, so Flight Plan follows them with his planner's maths and checks every 15 minutes whether his site changed them; if it did, the note says a pick may differ slightly until Flight Plan is updated. With Base only on, his own "Base" setting is used. You also see his call: move now, or stay because the gain doesn't clear his bar. If his planner can't run, you get his advice for the nearest lock size from our hourly run instead, and the note says so.

When you pick one, the knobs move to the closest match to that method so you can see roughly how it thinks. Turning a knob (other than Pools on JPN's) switches to Custom, which is our model again. A split that uses a network other than Base is signed here too, from Base (see other networks). If a pick is a pool our scanner has never read, the split is shown but not signed. Every pool row also shows its JPN rank (like "JPN #3") when it's in his top 40, whichever preset you use.

The dials

  • Pools. Auto (9 to 10 pools, at most 25% in one), or exactly 1 to 10, each pool then taking up to 100 ÷ N percent (see how many pools). 1 means one pool per lock, chosen across Base and the other networks after costs, with only the 10%-of-a-pool limit; with several locks, the biggest lock picks first and the rest may share its pool.
  • Fee lookback (how far back to trust fees). Each pool's fees are read over the last 12 hours, 24 hours, 2 days and 4 days. The knob sets how those reads are weighted: all the way down = the 4-day read only, the middle = the fitted mix the scanner uses (40% / 25% / 10% / 25%), all the way up = the last 12 hours only. Trust spikes (the small switch under the knobs) drops two safety rails: the cap on any one reading at 10x the pool's typical rate, and the rule that a fading pool (last 12 hours under 30% of its longest read) counts only at its 12-hour rate.
  • Avoid crowds (how much to expect others to pile into the same pools). Allocators pile into the pools that look best, so each pool's allocation is priced at a measured pile-on factor (fitted after every window, higher for crowded pools and small pools). The knob scales that factor: 1.0x = as measured; 0.5x assumes the crowd follows half as much; 1.5x assumes a heavier pile-on.
  • Count incentives. How much the incentives projects pay to a pool count next to its trading fees, in steps of 25%: Full = in full, Fees only = ignore incentives.
  • Base only (the small switch under the knobs) keeps one-pool picks on Base, for anyone who wants to compare or doesn't want to allocate on another network. It stays as you set it when you change presets.

The caps that protect you never move with the dial: your slice stays at most 10% of any pool, small pools stay capped, a pool whose allocation doubled since the last scan stays out, and the dry run and the signing guard are the same for every preset.

Move or stay

An allocation keeps earning until you change it, so moving only makes sense when the new split is clearly better. Flight Plan values your current allocation for the same window with the same model and the same dials as the suggested split (your own allocation is taken out of each pool first, so both see the pools as they would be without you), then compares:

  • Worth moving when the split beats staying by at least 3% and at least $0.25 a day (about $0.49 over a 47-hour window), after about $0.05 in network fees per lock.
  • Staying is about as good otherwise. You can still move; the page just tells you it barely pays.
  • Can't compare when more than a quarter of your Base allocation sits in pools the page doesn't price (a pool no longer in the scan). Locks allocated on another network are left out of both sides.

AERO that isn't allocated at all earns nothing, so any split beats it; the box says how much of yours is idle.

Why your split differs from someone else's

The split is sized to your locks. Your own AERO dilutes the pool and the caps scale with your size.

Example: a small pool has 200,000 AERO allocated to it and will pay allocators $100 this window.

A 1,000 AERO lockputs 5% here (50 AERO): 50 ÷ 200,050 of $100, about $0.025. Per AERO that's the pool's full rate, so small wallets happily use small, well-paid pools.
A 2,000,000 AERO walletcan put at most 10% of the pool here (20,000 AERO), 1% of the wallet: 20,000 ÷ 220,000 of $100, about $9.09. The rest has to go somewhere deeper.

Rank: your locks vs everyone

  • All your locks count as one: combined dollars over combined AERO, for now, 2 days, 7 days and lifetime.
  • Now: earned or forecast. Earned looks back: what locks actually earned over about the last 12 hours. Forecast looks ahead: what each lock's current allocation would pay at today's pool rates if nothing moved (each pool's fees over the last 4 days with spikes capped, plus incentives paying right now, divided by the pool's allocation, which already includes yours). Forecast is raw: rewards often land below or above it, and it can't see a crowd arriving. Every lock is ranked the same way, so the rank compares like with like.
  • Lifetime: everything a lock has earned since it first allocated: every reward it has claimed on every network (read from the claim events since sAERO launched on 10/21/2026) plus what is waiting now, all valued at today's prices, divided by the days since its first allocation and the whole lock's AERO. A lock that grew along the way is measured on its size today.
  • Every lock / Every wallet. Every lock compares you with each sAERO lock on its own. Every wallet groups all of an address's locks into one entry (a contract such as a vault counts as one wallet).
  • Size filter (10k+ to 1M+) ranks you among locks or wallets at least that big. It starts on your own size.
  • Whole-lock APR. Earnings are divided by everything a lock holds, including AERO parked and not allocated anywhere, because parked AERO earns nothing. When that differs from the APR on the allocated part alone, the allocated-part APR shows small underneath.
  • Average is weighted by AERO: every lock's (or wallet's) APR counts in proportion to its size, so it answers "what did the average AERO in this filter earn". A few small locks with a huge burst over a short window can't drag it around. Median is the middle lock, the typical one. Both cover locks (or wallets) over 100 AERO in the filter. Until 11/5/2026 the average was a plain average, each lock counted once.
  • Compare with: Autopilot USDC, Autopilot sAERO, 40acres. Public products that allocate on behalf of many lockers, each shown as one combined entry with the same math: whole-lock APR, allocated-part APR when it differs, rank, top %, and dollars per week per 1,000 AERO. Autopilot is Aero's own relay (a keeper picks its pools); 40acres is a third-party vault whose per-user accounts share one contract template, which is how they are recognised.

Earnings are read on chain for every lock (fees and incentives, claims bridged, every network) and valued at current prices. Rank data refreshes about every 3 hours.

By week, by day: you vs the average over time

  • By week follows Aero's epochs (Thursday 00:00 UTC to Thursday). By day runs midnight to midnight US Eastern. The choice is remembered on this device.
  • Each row: your APR for that bucket (all your locks as one), the average of every lock or wallet in your size filter, how far you sat from it, and your rank when your locks were in that bucket.
  • Today so far and This week so far run to the last every-lock read, which happens about every 3 hours; the row says when. Rewards land in lumps, so a running row swings until the bucket closes.
  • Why "Now" and "Today so far" differ. Both end at the same read, but "Now" starts about 12 hours back (its exact hours show under it) while "Today so far" starts at midnight. When most of a day's rewards land in one lump, as they often do in the morning, the shorter window divides that lump over fewer hours and shows a higher APR. Neither is wrong; they measure different hours.
  • The daily log reaches back about 8 days (the reads only keep that much), and a lock only has history from the day it was first tracked. Old days were measured across 2 or 3 reads and say so.

Earnings

  • APR 24h / 7d: fees and incentives each lock earned over the period, on the whole lock, at today's AERO price, annualized.
  • Earned: what the tracker has seen the lock earn since it first read it, with claims bridged (a claim doesn't reset the count) across every network. The reads keep about 8 days, so this covers at most that window; the Since date shows where the count starts.
  • Lifetime: every claim the lock has made on every network since sAERO launched, plus what is waiting now, at today's prices. The date under it is the lock's first allocation.
  • Unclaimed: what the last read saw waiting to be claimed, every network, in dollars.

The sweep: rewards to USDC

  1. Claim your rewards in the Aero app first (Flight Plan never claims).
  2. Scan. The page lists every token in the wallet with a route to USDC through Aero's new pools (the block explorer's token list, the Aero token universe, and any extra addresses you add), quotes each route live through Aero's Metaquoter, and picks the best.
  3. Dust and slippage. Tokens under the dust floor are skipped by default (you can tick them back); the minimum received is the quote minus your slippage setting. Tokens under $0.25 are marked "may revert" so one failing dust swap can't block the rest.
  4. Dry run simulates the approvals and the swap from your address and shows the USDC you would get. Nothing is sent.
  5. Sign. An exact-amount approve to the Metarouter for each token that needs one, then one Metarouter execute that sells them all, with the USDC paid to your own wallet. A wallet that supports batching signs it once; otherwise one signature per call.
  6. A red warning on the swap is expected. Wallets like Rabby preview a swap as one token in and one token out. The sweep sells several tokens in one swap, so the preview compares a single token with the whole USDC total and can flag a big "loss" or "slippage" in red. Check the balance change at the bottom of the wallet screen instead: every token you ticked goes out, and the USDC coming in should match the dry run. If the balance change doesn't match, reject it.
  7. Every network. The scan checks Base, Arbitrum, OP, Ethereum, Robinhood, Ink and Arc at once. When rewards sit on more than one, a button per network shows what each holds, and each one is its own sweep, signed on that network with a little of its gas (gas on each network). Robinhood's pools settle in USDG (Global Dollar, a dollar stablecoin), so a Robinhood sweep pays out USDG.
  8. Tokens sharing a pool. When two tokens route through the same pool, the first swap moves its price for the second. The dry run rehearses them in order first and sets the later token's minimum from what it really gets, so one tight minimum can't sink the whole sweep.
  9. The guard. Every call is checked against an allowlist before it reaches your wallet: only approve(Metarouter, exactly the balance) on a token the scan found, or execute() on the Metarouter.

When the crowd moves

Every sAERO allocation since launch is read from the chain. The chart shows sAERO moved per 2 hours over the last 72 hours, sAERO coming off its 47-hour cooldown over the next 72 hours, and how much of it is likely to move (from how quickly locks have historically moved after their window opened). The shaded band is when the Aero team's incentive batch usually lands; your own lock windows are pinned with a dashed line.

The edge: an allocation keeps earning past its window until you change it, so waiting for a big wave to move (its dilution is then known) and for the team's batch to land is free.

What actually wins

Flight Plan collects nothing from the people who use it: there is no server. It doesn't need to. Every sAERO allocation and every lock's earnings are on the chain, and the scanner reads all of them every 3 hours. So instead of learning from a few users, the page learns from every lock.

  • Each ranked lock is grouped by what it did: how many pools it allocates to right now (1, 2 to 3, 4 to 6, 7 or more), how many times it moved in the last 7 days, and whether its allocation sits mostly in fee-led pools (fees out-pay incentives), stream-led pools, or a mix. Pool kind comes from the latest Base scan; allocations on other networks are left out of the mix share.
  • Each group shows how many locks it holds and its 7-day whole-lock APR: the average weighted by AERO, and the median, the typical lock in that group. The size filter works like the rank card's.
  • Your own group is marked "you" (your pools and your fee/stream mix, from your current allocation).
  • A snapshot of the table is kept at the end of every Aero week, so the view will reach back past the scanner's 8-day memory as weeks accumulate. The presets on the strategy dial get tuned from this table, not from guesses.

Counts and averages only are published: nothing per lock or per wallet leaves the scanner.

How each strategy did (the backtest)

Every 3 hours the scanner replays every strategy on its own past scans. For each past hour on a 3-hour grid it rebuilds exactly what the planner knew at that moment (pool scans up to then, the incentive programs seen by then, the self-tuned settings as they were then), makes each strategy's split with this same planner.js, and then pays that split from what the pools really paid over the next 47 hours: per scan, (allocator fees + incentives) x your AERO / (the pool's actual allocation at that scan + your AERO). Real pile-ons are included, and nothing from the future leaks into a plan.

  • Strategies: Balanced, Steady and Aggressive as on the dial, Aggressive held to Base, exactly 1, 3, 6 and 10 pools, and the old spike-chaser (the last 12 hours only, spikes trusted). Lock sizes 1k, 30k, 100k and 1M AERO.
  • JPN's and Cypher's picks can't be rebuilt from old scans: their tools only show today's advice. So their picks are recorded live (JPN's every 3 hours, Cypher's every hour, Base picks for a ladder of lock sizes) and graded exactly like the rest, but only at starts where a recording exists from before the start (no peeking). Their rows fill in over time; JPN's go back to Nov 2, Cypher's start Nov 6.
  • Other networks: the best single pool on each network, after the cost of allocating there, against the best on Base at the same moment. Ink, Robinhood and Arc were scanned once a day until Nov 5 (every 3 hours since), so their early figures are rough.
  • Checked: replaying real locks' allocations the same way lands within about 0 to 15% of what the chain says they earned (slightly high).
  • "Beat Balanced" counts separate 2-day stretches, not hours: starts 3 hours apart share most of their 47 hours, so a week of data is only 3 to 5 separate windows. Treat small differences as noise.

Calibration. On past data the planner's forecasts ran high, and worse, they barely predicted which windows would pay more: payouts stayed in a narrow band whatever the forecast said, and settings that trust the last 12 hours or fresh spikes forecast the biggest numbers while paying less. So the page pulls every expected, low and high figure toward what your setting typically paid. Per 1,000 AERO: shown = typical payout + slope x (this forecast - typical forecast), where the slope is how far that setting's forecasts actually tracked its payouts (often zero), and never more than the forecast itself. Presets use their own measurements; Custom dials are snapped to the nearest of 200 tested combinations (fee lookback 0, 25, 50, 75 or 100%, trust spikes on or off, avoid crowds 0 to 2x, count incentives 0 or 100%, one pool or several). A pool on another network keeps its full cost. Refit every 3 hours; "move or stay" scales both sides the same way. The card lists each setting's typical payout.

Gas on each network

An allocation on another network pays its rewards on that network, so sweeping them is a transaction on that network, paid for with that network's own gas token. Each network sweeps on its own: tap its button above the token list, and your wallet is asked to switch to it (and to add it first if it has never seen it).

Setting up gas

  1. Same address everywhere. All of these networks use the same kind of address, so your wallet's address on Arbitrum or Robinhood is the one you already have. You are sending gas to yourself.
  2. Bridge a little ETH from Base. A bridge such as Relay moves ETH from Base to any of these networks in one transaction, usually within seconds. Choose Base as "from", the network you need as "to", your own address as the recipient, and about $1 (about $5 for Ethereum). Relay is a separate service, not part of this page; check the address bar says relay.link before you sign anything there.
  3. Arc is different: its gas is USDC, not ETH. Bridge USDC to Arc (Relay lists Arc too). On Arc the USDC you hold is also your gas, so once you have some, each sweep tops it up.
  4. Re-scan. The "needs gas" mark on that network's button disappears once there is enough.

Or tap Get gas

When a network is short, the sweep shows Get gas: bridge ~$1 of ETH from Base (and Get gas on all N networks when several are). The page asks Relay for a quote, shows what goes in and what arrives, checks it, and rehearses the deposit on Base. One signature on Base per network, or one in total if your wallet signs batches. Relay usually delivers within seconds; if it can't, it refunds you on Base. You need that ETH (plus a little gas) on Base first.

The page won't bridge to a smart-contract wallet (like a Safe) on a network where that wallet doesn't exist, because the gas could be stuck there.

The page checks before you sign. If the wallet is short on a network, the sweep says so in plain words, for example "To swap ARB on Arbitrum you need about 0.00002 ETH (~$0.05) for gas on Arbitrum. This wallet has 0 ETH there", and Sign stays off. The dry run still works without gas, so you can see what a sweep would pay before funding it.

Aero's Metarouter and Metaquoter have the same address on every network, and each network's sweep goes through the same guard as Base's: exact-amount approvals to the Metarouter, one swap paid to your own address.

What it signs, and never signs

Connecting shares only your address. With the owning wallet connected, Flight Plan can ask for exactly four kinds of signature:

  1. An allocation. One allocate call to the Aero Voter on Base for a lock your wallet owns. On Base it carries 0 ETH. The guard checks, before every send: the To address is the Voter; the call is allocate; the lock id is the one shown and your account owns it; the refund address is you; every dispatch is Base-only with zero value and zero gas limit; the gauges are exactly the plan's; the amounts add up to the whole stake. It is rebuilt from the plan and checked again against a fresh chain read (owner, window, booked amount) right before your wallet opens.
    When the split uses another network the same call carries that network's message fee as ETH (how). Its own guard checks everything above, plus: only Aero's networks; exactly the AERO the plan moves to each network, the plan's pools and amounts on each, every network's vote adding up to its budget; the "free this AERO" marker only where the plan frees AERO, for exactly that amount; Base entries with no fee; each other network with a sane gas limit and a fee no higher than Aero's own quote + 20% (plus that network's fixed return cost when AERO comes back); the ETH sent equal to the fees and never over 0.03 ETH. The whole rehearsal runs again with fresh fees right before your wallet opens.
  2. A sweep approval. approve on a reward token the scan found in your wallet, spender = Aero's Metarouter, amount = exactly your balance. Never unlimited.
  3. A sweep swap. One Metarouter execute that sells those tokens for USDC (USDG on Robinhood) with the minimum you set, paid to your own address, on the network the tokens are on.
  4. A gas bridge, only when you tap Get gas. depositNative on Relay's Base deposit contract 0x4cd00e387622c35bddb9b4c962c136462338bc31, with about $1 of ETH ($5 for Ethereum, hard cap 0.006 ETH) as value. The guard checks the To address, the method, that the depositor is you, the exact amount, and that Relay's quote delivers that network's gas token to your own address; then it dry-runs the deposit on Base. More

Never

  • An approval on sAERO, or setApprovalForAll on anything. Your locks cannot be moved by this page: allocate moves your allocation, not tokens, and there is no code path that approves the escrow.
  • A transfer, a claim, a withdrawal, a merge, an unlimited approval, a message signature (personal_sign / typed data), or any ETH value except an allocation's quoted network fee and that capped gas bridge.
  • Anything without a passing dry run first, and nothing in paste-only mode (no wallet connected).

Worst case, if you ignore every check: a swap that fills at your slippage limit, or a poor pool pick for one 47-hour window. No lock can be lost.

Check the address bar says flight-plan.app. A copy on another address can be anything. If any page claiming to be Flight Plan asks for a signature that doesn't match the three above, close it.

Never sign blind

  1. Dry run first, every time, so you know exactly what your allocation (or the swap) should become.
  2. Network is Base, the To address is the Voter (an allocation) or the Metarouter (a sweep) or a reward token (an approval), and the value is 0 ETH (an allocation that uses another network: the fee shown under its dry run). The addresses are below; compare the start and the end.
  3. Your wallet's preview should show your sAERO lock re-allocated and no tokens moving (an allocation), or minus the reward tokens and plus USDC (a sweep).
Reject anything that says "setApprovalForAll", "approve" on sAERO, "transfer", an amount larger than the row, or a value other than the one the dry run showed.

Hardware wallets

A Keystone, Ledger or similar device shows allocate and execute as raw data: it cannot decode them, so the device screen alone can't tell you what the call does. What you can check on the device: the network (Base), the To address (compare its start and end with the addresses below), the method selector (0xf02e634e for allocate), and 0 ETH. What tells you the content is right: the dry run on this page (the chain's own answer), the "What you will sign" list under each dry run, and your software wallet's simulation (Rabby shows the lock re-allocated and no token moves). Approvals decode normally: check the spender is the Metarouter and the amount is your balance.

Other networks

Aero runs on several networks (OP, Arbitrum, Ethereum, Ink, Robinhood, Arc) and a lock can allocate on any of them. With one pool per lock (Aggressive, or Pools = 1), Flight Plan weighs pools on every Aero network after what allocating there costs each window, unless the Base only switch is on. JPN's and Cypher's picks can use other networks too (see their section). Their allocations come from the last scan of that network (every 3 hours; a read older than 6 hours is left out), and the row says how old it is. Multi-pool splits stay on Base.

How Flight Plan signs them

The way Aero built it, every allocation is a Base transaction to the same Voter, even one that lands on another network. The other network needs no transaction, no gas and no approval from you: the vote travels there as a message over Hyperlane, Aero's bridge. That message has a fee, paid as ETH on the same Base call. Flight Plan reads the fee from Aero's own bridge contract (the same number the Aero app sends), adds 10% in case prices tick, and the bridge refunds whatever isn't used to you. Typical: about $0.40 to $1 per network on OP and Arbitrum, $1 to $2 on Ink, Robinhood and Arc, $2 to $4 on Ethereum.

Moving AERO between networks takes two signatures. Aero books the AERO a network gets before it books the votes, so freeing AERO and sending it can't share one call:

  1. Step 1 frees the AERO that's leaving a network (with that network's remaining vote, if any). Freeing on Base is instant. Freeing on another network sends a message there, and the AERO comes back to Base a few minutes later.
  2. Step 2 sends the AERO to the networks gaining it and votes there. When step 1 freed only Base AERO, your wallet asks for step 2 right after step 1 confirms. When AERO is coming back from another network, the card says so and Flight Plan checks every 20 seconds; step 2 unlocks once it has landed.

A network whose amount and pools already match the plan (within 1%) is left alone: no fee, and its clock keeps running. Each network has its own 47-hour clock; a step waits until every network it votes on is open.

The dry run covers both halves

The Base call is rehearsed on Base. Then the exact message it would send is replayed on the other network, into Aero's own contracts there, and the lock's vote is read back: its budget, every pool's amount, nothing else left over. That also measures the gas the message needs there (the limit sent is that x 1.4, plus a margin). If the other network would refuse it (its clock still running, a pool switched off), the dry run says so before anything is signed.

While a move is landing

After you sign, the vote usually lands on the other network in 1 to 3 minutes; AERO coming back to Base takes a few minutes more. The card shows "landing on OP" and Flight Plan won't plan that lock again until it has landed, so the same move is never sent twice. You can close the page: it remembers the move in your browser and checks the chain when you come back.

The sweep works on every one of these networks too (gas on each network): rewards from an allocation on another network are paid there.

Data and freshness

Pool estimates are published every hour; ranks, history and earnings every 3 hours; the crowd chart every hour. How much AERO each pool has allocated is read live when you open the page. The footer shows how old the pool data is, and the page warns when it's more than 3 hours old.

Open source under the MIT license. The planning math runs in your browser (planner.js); the allocate call is built and checked by allocate-lib.js; sweep routes come from sweep-routes.js and are re-quoted live. Prices from DefiLlama. Not financial advice: estimates can be wrong, and pools can change fast.
